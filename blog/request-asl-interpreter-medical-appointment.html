How to Request an ASL Interpreter for a Medical Appointment in San Diego

July 25, 2026

The appointment is booked. Now the part that too often goes wrong: making sure a qualified interpreter is actually in the room when you get there.

Whether you are a Deaf patient or the office staff scheduling the visit, the process is the same in spirit: ask early, be specific, and put it in writing. Here is how it works in practice.

Step One: Tell the Provider When You Book

The interpreter request goes to the healthcare provider — the clinic, hospital, or practice — because under the ADA the provider is responsible for arranging and paying for effective communication. Say it plainly at booking: “I am Deaf and will need a qualified ASL interpreter for this appointment.” Ask them to note it in the chart, and ask who is responsible for confirming the booking. A follow-up email creates a record and concentrates minds.

What the Scheduler Needs to Know

Date, time, expected length, the type of appointment (a physical, a specialist consult, a procedure with consent forms), and any preference you have — for example, an interpreter experienced in mental health settings, or a preference for on-site over video. The more the interpreter can prepare, the better the appointment goes.

If the Office Pushes Back

“Can’t you bring someone?” or “we’ll write notes back and forth” are common — and for most medical discussions, neither is legally adequate. Family members are not qualified interpreters, and the ADA specifically restricts relying on them. A calm, firm sentence usually resolves it: “Under the ADA, effective communication for a medical appointment means a qualified interpreter, provided by the practice.” If it does not, ask for the office manager, and know that complaints can be filed with the Department of Justice — though in our experience, most San Diego practices simply need to be pointed in the right direction.

On-Site or Video?

For routine visits and telehealth, video remote interpreting can work well and is often faster to arrange. For high-stakes settings — surgery consults, labor and delivery, emergency care, difficult diagnoses — an interpreter physically in the room is the better standard, and it is reasonable to insist on one.

For Recurring Care, Ask for Consistency

Dialysis, physical therapy, prenatal visits, ongoing therapy: request the same interpreter as a standing series. Continuity means the interpreter already knows your history, your signing style, and the clinical context — and you are not re-explaining yourself every visit.
